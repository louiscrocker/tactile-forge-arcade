How a tornado works

1

Warm meets cold

Hot, damp air rushes up from the Gulf and slides under cold, dry air coming over the Rockies. The warm air wants to rise — fast.

2

The wind shears

Wind near the ground blows one way, wind higher up blows another. That difference rolls the air into an invisible horizontal tube, like a rolling pin lying in the sky.

3

The updraft tips it upright

A thunderstorm's updraft grabs the spinning tube and stands it on end. Now the whole storm is rotating — that's a mesocyclone, and the storm is a supercell.

4

It tightens and speeds up

As the spinning column stretches and narrows, it spins faster — the same reason an ice skater speeds up by pulling their arms in. This is conservation of angular momentum.

5

The funnel appears

Pressure inside the vortex drops so low that water vapour condenses into cloud. That white funnel is not the tornado — it's just the part you can see. The wind extends well beyond it.

6

The debris cloud

When the circulation reaches the ground it lifts dirt, fence posts, shingles — and yes, occasionally livestock. Debris is what makes a tornado dangerous, and what surveyors read afterwards.

What the app is showing you

  • The wind field is a Rankine combined vortex: wind speed climbs straight up from zero at the centre to a maximum at the funnel wall, then falls off outside it. That's why the very middle is eerily calm.
  • Inflow spirals inwards near the ground, then turns into a violent updraft at the funnel wall — the reason objects get carried upward instead of just sideways.
  • Suction vortices are smaller whirls orbiting inside a big tornado. They're why one house is flattened while the one next door keeps its porch light on.
  • Damage is staged: gutters → shingles → roof deck → walls → swept to the slab. Every object has its own failure wind speed.
