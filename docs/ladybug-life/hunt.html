Ladybug Scavenger HuntTake the game outside

Find each of these in a real garden or park. Look on the tips of new shoots, under leaves and along stems — that is where the aphids are, and where everything that eats aphids lives too. Tick the box when you spot one.

Explorer's name:

An aphid colonyTiny green, black or yellow pear-shaped bugs crowded on a soft new stem or under a leaf.
An adult ladybugCount the spots! Is it a seven-spot, a two-spot, or something else? What colour is it?
A ladybug larvaLooks like a tiny grey-black alligator with orange spots. Usually right next to the aphids. Do not squish it!
A pupaAn orange-and-black blob glued to a leaf by its tail. It might twitch if you get close. Leave it be — a ladybug is being built inside.
A shed skinA pale, empty, papery larva skin stuck to a leaf or stem: proof that a larva molted here.
Ladybug eggsA cluster of tiny yellow ovals standing on end under a leaf, near aphids. Bonus: come back in a few days and see if they hatched.
Ants visiting aphidsAnts walking up and down a stem to an aphid colony. They are farming the aphids for sweet honeydew and will chase ladybugs away.
A leaf with sticky honeydewShiny, sticky spots on a leaf below an aphid colony. Sometimes it goes black with sooty mould.
A plant aphids loveRoses, beans, milkweed, nasturtiums or the soft tips of almost anything. Check the newest growth first.
A cosy winter crackA sheltered gap in a wall, log, fence or window frame where ladybugs might huddle together in winter. In autumn, look for a group!
Explorer tips: aphids and ladybugs both like the soft new tips of plants, so check there first. Turn leaves over gently. Take a photo instead of touching. A magnifying glass makes the larva's spikes and the aphid's tail-pipes easy to see. Best months: late spring and summer.
