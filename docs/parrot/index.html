    How to play

    1

    In the hollow

    Push the arrows to wiggle out of the egg. When Mom or Dad comes to the door with food, press Space to beg. Every meal grows your feathers.

    2

    Your name

    Your parents give you a call of your own: three arrows. Watch, then press the same arrows. That is your name. Press E to call it, any time. If a toucan looks in, call!

    3

    The first flight

    With all your feathers, press ↑ to climb to the door. Press Space again and again to flap. Then ↑ to jump!

    4

    Flying

    The arrows fly. Hold ↑ (or tap Space) to flap higher. Hold ↓ by a branch to land on it. On a branch, ← → walk along it and ↑ takes off.

    5

    Lessons

    Follow the arrow to your family. Land by a fig and press Space to eat. At the clay lick, eat clay. Crack a palm nut: Space when the ring is green. Each evening, fly to the tall kapok tree to sleep.

    6

    Eagles and mist

    If an eagle comes to the clay lick, fly up with the flock. If a hawk-eagle comes over the trees, fly into the leaves. Lost in the mist? Press E to call, and your family calls back. Nothing ever hurts you.

    7

    A friend for life

    When you are grown, land by the macaw that sits alone at the clay lick and preen it: Space on green, gently. Now you fly wing to wing.

    8

    A hollow of your own

    Land by a hole and press Space to look in. Most are taken! In the empty one, she lays the eggs and keeps them warm, and he brings food. Then give your chick a name.

    For the grown-ups: wild parrots stay wild. The game shows scarlet macaws as they live in the rainforest of south-east Peru: a nest in a hollow tree, parents that feed from the crop, a first flight at about three months, clay licks, pairs that stay together, and too few hollows to go round. It also shows people who watch from a distance and scientists who climb to nests and put the chicks straight back. Taking parrots from the wild for the pet trade is one reason some kinds are rare, so the game says plainly that a wild parrot belongs in the forest. Read-aloud uses this device's own voices, and the microphone and every link to another website ask a grown-up first. The name game rests on a real finding: wild green-rumped parrotlet chicks learn their own calls from the calls their parents make. For macaws that is what scientists think, and the game says so. Time is hugely compressed: a real macaw takes years to grow up.
