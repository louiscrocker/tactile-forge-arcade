🕸️ Web spotting

Spiders build wherever it is dry and quiet. Go and look for webs with a grown-up. Take this sheet and a torch.

LOOK. DO NOT TOUCH. TELL A GROWN-UP.

The spotter's rules

  1. I look with my eyes, not with my hands.
  2. I do not put my hand where I can not see: not under a pot, not in a woodpile, not in an old boot.
  3. I do not poke a web or a spider with a stick.
  4. If I see a shiny black spider with a red mark, I step back and tell a grown-up.
  5. Grown-ups wear gloves to move wood, pots and boxes.

Can you find these webs? Tick each one.

A wheel webRound, with spokes, like a bike wheel. An orb weaver made it.
A tangle webMessy threads in a corner, low down. Cobweb spiders (and widows) make these.
A sheet webFlat, like a little hammock, on grass or a bush.
A funnel webA flat sheet with a tube at the back where the spider waits.
A thread to the groundOne tight line going straight down. Is the bottom sticky? Do not test it: just look!
An egg sacA little ball or bag of silk, hanging in a web.
A shed skinA pale, empty spider shape. The spider grew out of it.
A wrapped parcelA little white bundle in a web: a spider's snack.
A web with dewLook early in the morning. Every thread shows.

For grown-ups. Black widows (Latrodectus) live across much of North America and in many warm parts of the world, in dry, sheltered places close to the ground. They are shy and bite only when pressed or squeezed, but a bite can make a person very sick, and children are affected more. If you think someone has been bitten, call your doctor or, in the United States, Poison Control on 1-800-222-1222; get medical care right away for a child. Most spiders you will find are harmless and useful. This sheet goes with the game Black Widow: Thread by Thread.
