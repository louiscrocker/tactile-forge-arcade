    STARFISHTIDE BY TIDEA sea star's life on the rocky shore

    Be an ochre sea star on the wild Olympic coast! Hatch as a tiny see-through larva and swim with the plankton. Come back to the shore, hold on to a pink rock and turn into a star smaller than a pinhead. Crawl on hundreds of tube feet, ride the tide up the rocks to eat barnacles and mussels, hide in a tide pool before you dry, hold on tight when the big waves crash… make room on the shore for new life, and start the next generation.

    How to play

    1

    Hatch

    You start as one of millions of eggs that your mother let go on a spring evening, standing tall on the mussel bed. Push any arrow to wiggle and hatch.

    2

    A larva in the plankton

    You do not look like a star yet: you are a see-through larva that swims with bands of tiny hairs. Swim with the arrows and bump into the little green plants (diatoms and algae) to eat them. Press E to swim fast. Food is near the top at night. Herring, comb jellies, a grey whale and a gull never hurt you: you just tumble. Eat and grow twice, and three sticky arms grow at your front.

    3

    Settle on a pink rock

    Follow the arrow back to the shore and sink down. Find a pink rock with nobody on it (crowded ones are packed with mussels) and press Space to hold on. You change into a tiny five-armed star!

    4

    A tiny star

    Crawl with the arrows and press Space by the baby barnacles to eat them. When you are full, go into a tide pool or a crack (or stay by your pink rock) and press Space to rest and grow.

    5

    The tide

    Twice a day the sea climbs up the shore and falls back. When the tide is in, crawl up the rocks: you can only eat under water. When it goes out, watch the 💧 wet meter: get into a tide pool, a crack or under rockweed before you dry. Too dry? You hold your water in until a wave splashes you. The 🌊 readout shows the tide coming in (▲) or going out (▼).

    6

    Hold on tight!

    A big wave, a gull, a sea otter or a red rock crab? Press E to hold on tight with all your tube feet (keeping still helps too). Nothing can budge you; if you were not holding on, you just roll a little way down the rock. A sunflower star? Crawl away! A child tide-pooling may lift you up gently to look, and put you back.

    7

    Mussels and the keystone

    Bigger now, eat limpets, snails, chitons and then mussels: press Space and pull, pull, pull until the shell opens, and push your stomach in! Eat enough mussels and bare rock opens up: anemones, barnacles, seaweed and more move in. A scientist may come to count you: press Space to hold still.

    8

    Winter and spring

    The winter storm brings wave after wave: press E each time one comes. In spring the sea stars gather. Crawl to the ones with hearts and press Space to say hi, then stand tall and press Space each time the wave glows: girls let millions of eggs go, boys let a milky cloud go.

    For the grown-ups: read-aloud uses this device's own voices, and the microphone and every link to another website ask a grown-up first. The ochre sea star's life is real, simplified for play: spring spawning with millions of eggs and a milky cloud let go into the water, standing tall on the arm tips; the swimming bipinnaria and the brachiolaria with its three sticky arms, weeks in the plankton eating diatoms; settling on coralline-crusted rock and turning into a tiny five-armed star; tube feet worked by sea water; eating at high tide, barnacles first, then limpets, snails, chitons and California mussels, pulled open and eaten with the stomach pushed out; surviving low tide in pools and cracks; holding on through waves; gulls, sea otters and the sunflower star; turban snails that tumble away; the eyespots, the spines, the sieve plate and an arm tip that regrows; the keystone (Robert Paine's experiment on the Washington coast); and scientists who count sea stars every year. The five colours are real colours of one species. The tides are faster than real ones, and nothing in the game is ever hurt.
